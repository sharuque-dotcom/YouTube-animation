# Episode 2: The CEO, the Chips and the Alleged Spy

Toppled Giants · narration as recorded · facts as of 10 November 2026

**[c_email]**
On Sunday, November 8th, 2026, the most important man in artificial intelligence sent an email to his employees.
"I was unfaithful to my wife," he wrote. "My actions hurt her and our children. There is no excuse for what I did."
The man was Jensen Huang, the founder and CEO of Nvidia.

**[c_twist]**
But this was not just a story about an affair.
The woman at the centre of it, according to reports, had been watched by American intelligence, who suspected she could be working for China.
And her company had bought billions of dollars' worth of Nvidia's chips.

**[c_q]**
So is this a love story, a spy story, or a story about the most valuable chips on Earth?
Maybe all three.

**[title]**

**[chapter1]**

**[rise]**
Jensen Huang was born in Taiwan in 1963, and moved to the United States as a child.
In 1993, he and two friends started a company to make graphics chips for video games. The plan was famously sketched out in a Denny's restaurant.
They called it Nvidia.

**[ai]**
For years, Nvidia's chips made video games look better.
Then researchers discovered the same chips were perfect for training artificial intelligence.
When the AI boom arrived, almost every major AI system in the world needed Nvidia.

**[value]**
By late 2026, Nvidia was worth roughly 4.5 trillion dollars, one of the most valuable companies ever.
And Jensen Huang, in his trademark leather jacket, became one of the most powerful businessmen alive.

**[chapter2]**

**[wall]**
But Nvidia's chips aren't just products anymore.
Governments now treat them like strategic weapons.
Since 2022, the United States has restricted the export of its most advanced AI chips to China, worried they could power China's military and its own AI race.
China is a huge market, so the rules have cost Nvidia billions.

**[leak]**
As the wall went up, one question kept coming back.
Were the chips finding a way around it?
Investigators and lawmakers started looking at Southeast Asia, especially Singapore and Malaysia, as possible routes for chips to reach Chinese customers.
Nvidia has always said it follows the rules.

**[chapter3]**

**[mega]**
Which brings us to a company called Megaspeed.
Megaspeed is a cloud computing company based in Singapore.
According to reports, it began as a spin-off from a Chinese gaming and cloud firm called 7Road, and was later sold to an offshore company linked to a woman named Alice Huang.

**[alice]**
Alice Huang had worked as a television reporter in China.
From March to November 2023, she was Megaspeed's managing director.
Huang is a common surname. The two are not reported to be related.

**[chips]**
Megaspeed became one of Nvidia's big customers.
The New York Times reported that it bought more than 4.8 billion dollars of Nvidia chips over two years.
Investigators are now examining whether some of those chips were diverted to China.
The Times also reported that the Justice Department has opened a criminal inquiry into the company.

**[deny]**
Megaspeed denies all of it.
It calls the reports "misleading information and incorrect insinuations", and says inspections by American and Malaysian officials, and by Nvidia, found no evidence of any violations.

**[chapter4]**

**[report]**
On October 29th, The New York Times published a story that changed everything.
It reported that American intelligence agencies had been watching a person close to Jensen Huang, because they believed she could be a Chinese intelligence asset.
The concern, according to the report, was that she could help Beijing gain information about Nvidia, and about a CEO with access to the President of the United States.

**[more]**
The Wall Street Journal reported that intelligence services in the United States, Singapore and Taiwan all had reason to suspect her.
The Financial Times reported that the relationship was romantic.

**[nvreply]**
Nvidia pushed back.
It said Huang talks to thousands of customers every year, and that the chip diversion allegations had been "thoroughly investigated and debunked".

**[chapter5]**

**[congress]**
But the story would not go away.
Last week, three Republican committee chairmen in the House asked Huang to brief them behind closed doors: from the Foreign Affairs Committee, the China Committee, and the Intelligence Committee.
Democrats called for answers too.

**[confess]**
Then came Sunday's email.
Huang admitted the affair, and said he supported an investigation and would cooperate fully.
Nvidia's board announced that independent directors, with outside lawyers, would investigate, including whether Huang had been the target of a foreign intelligence effort.
The board said it had no reason to believe that national security or Nvidia's secrets were compromised.

**[market]**
On Monday, Nvidia's stock fell about 4 percent.
President Trump called Huang "an honorable guy", and added that the FBI was "looking into it in terms of something else".
Nvidia says Huang will stay on as CEO, without interruption.

**[chapter6]**

**[known]**
So, what do we actually know?
Huang has admitted an affair. That part is confirmed.
The spying claims come from anonymous intelligence sources quoted in news reports.
No one has been charged. Alice Huang has not commented publicly.
And Megaspeed says it did nothing wrong.

**[next]**
What happens next matters.
Nvidia's board is investigating. Lawmakers want a briefing. And investigators are still looking at where Megaspeed's chips went.

**[outro]**
For decades, the biggest risk to a company like Nvidia was a better chip.
Now, in a world where chips are power, the biggest risk might be something far more human.
This story is still developing, and we'll update you as it does.
