# Episode 1: The Company Called "Truth" Was India's Biggest Lie

- **Topic:** Satyam Computer Services fraud (2009)
- **Target length:** ~11 minutes (~1,650 words of narration at ~150 words/min)
- **Thumbnail idea:** A cracked golden trophy labeled "BEST GOVERNANCE 2008" next to a burning ledger. Text: **"₹7,000 CRORE LIE"**
- **Format:** `VISUAL` = what the animation shows. `VO` = narration.

---

## COLD OPEN (0:00 – 0:45)

**VISUAL:** Black screen. A single typed letter fades in, dated *7 January 2009*. Camera pushes in on one line.

**VO:**
On the morning of January 7th, 2009, the chairman of one of India's biggest companies sat down and wrote a letter.

In it, he admitted something nobody believed was possible.

The billions of rupees his company said it had in the bank? Almost all of it… didn't exist.

**VISUAL:** Stock ticker crashing red. Headlines flying past.

**VO:**
By evening, the company's share price had crashed nearly 80 percent. Fifty thousand employees didn't know if they still had jobs. And India's entire IT industry, the pride of the nation, was suddenly under suspicion.

The strangest part? The company's name was *Satyam*.

In Sanskrit, Satyam means… **truth.**

**VISUAL:** Title card: **PAPER EMPIRES** → *"The Company Called Truth"*

---

## ACT 1: THE PERFECT SUCCESS STORY (0:45 – 2:45)

**VISUAL:** Map of India, zoom to Hyderabad. Year counter: 1987.

**VO:**
The story starts in Hyderabad in 1987. A businessman named B. Ramalinga Raju, from a family that had worked in textiles and real estate, starts a small software company with his brother.

They call it Satyam Computer Services.

**VISUAL:** A tiny office grows into a glass tower. A globe spins with lines connecting India to the US and Europe.

**VO:**
The timing is perfect. The world is going digital, and Western companies have discovered something: Indian engineers can write the same code for a fraction of the cost.

Then comes the Y2K panic. Every company on earth needs old software fixed before the year 2000, and Indian IT firms are flooded with work.

**VISUAL:** "NYSE" stamp lands on the screen. Year counter: 2001.

**VO:**
Satyam grows explosively. In 2001, it lists on the New York Stock Exchange. By 2008 it has over 50,000 employees, offices around the world, and clients that include a huge list of Fortune 500 companies.

It's one of the "Big Four" of Indian IT, alongside TCS, Infosys and Wipro.

**VISUAL:** A golden trophy rotates slowly. Label: *Golden Peacock Global Award for Excellence in Corporate Governance, 2008.*

**VO:**
And in 2008, Satyam wins a prestigious award… for excellence in corporate governance.

In other words, for being honest.

Remember that trophy. We'll come back to it.

---

## ACT 2: THE SMALL LIE (2:45 – 4:45)

**VISUAL:** Two bars side by side: "Real profit" and "Reported profit." They start almost equal.

**VO:**
Here's what nobody outside knew.

Years earlier, Satyam's real profits had started falling a little short of what the market expected. The gap was small. A few numbers adjusted. Just enough to keep the share price happy.

The plan, presumably, was simple: next year would be better, and the gap would quietly close.

**VISUAL:** Year counter ticks forward. The "Reported" bar keeps growing; the "Real" bar barely moves. The gap glows red.

**VO:**
But it never closed. Every year, the fake profit had to match the fake profit from the year before, *plus* growth. Every lie needed a bigger lie to cover it.

And fake profits create a problem: if you claim you earned money, that money has to be *somewhere*.

**VISUAL:** Animated bank statements and fixed-deposit receipts being printed, stamped, and filed.

**VO:**
So the money appeared on paper. Cash balances. Bank deposits. Interest earned on those deposits. According to investigators, thousands of fake invoices were created for work that was never done, and bank documents were forged to show money that was never there.

On paper, Satyam was sitting on a mountain of cash.

In reality, the mountain was made of paper.

---

## ACT 3: THE ESCAPE PLAN (4:45 – 6:45)

**VISUAL:** The word **SATYAM** on screen. Letters slowly rearrange in reverse: **MAYTAS**.

**VO:**
By 2008, the hole had grown so big it couldn't be hidden much longer. Raju needed a way out.

And it seems he found one in a company called Maytas.

Notice something? Maytas is Satyam… spelled backwards.

**VISUAL:** Two buildings: Satyam tower and Maytas construction sites. An arrow labeled "$1.6 billion" moves from Satyam to Maytas.

**VO:**
Maytas Properties and Maytas Infra were real-estate and infrastructure companies controlled by Raju's family.

On December 16th, 2008, Satyam announced it would buy them for about 1.6 billion dollars.

On the surface, it made no sense. Why would a software company buy construction businesses?

But look at it from the inside. If Satyam "spent" its fake cash buying real companies, the fake money would disappear into a deal… and be replaced by real buildings and land.

The hole would be filled. Forever.

**VISUAL:** Angry investor icons swarm. Share price arrow plunges.

**VO:**
It almost worked. But investors were furious. Why was the company spending shareholders' money on the founder's family businesses?

Satyam's shares crashed. Within about a day, the deal was cancelled.

**VISUAL:** A stamp slams down: *World Bank: BANNED.* Board-member icons disappear one by one.

**VO:**
Then the hits kept coming. Days later, it emerged that the World Bank had banned Satyam from its contracts. Independent directors began resigning.

The escape door had slammed shut.

And Raju was trapped inside his own lie.

---

## ACT 4: THE LETTER (6:45 – 8:30)

**VISUAL:** Back to the letter from the cold open. Lines highlight as they're read.

**VO:**
Which brings us back to January 7th, 2009.

Raju's letter to the board was addressed to the directors and the stock exchanges. And it was a full confession.

**VISUAL:** Numbers slam onto the screen one by one.

**VO:**
The cash and bank balances: overstated by more than 5,000 crore rupees. That's over a billion dollars that simply did not exist.

Interest on deposits that didn't exist: fake.

Money owed by customers: inflated.

Even the latest quarter: the company claimed operating margins of around 24 percent. The real figure was about 3 percent.

**VISUAL:** A tiger, drawn in ink, prowling in a circle. A tiny figure rides on its back.

**VO:**
In the letter, Raju described his situation with a line that would become famous:

*"It was like riding a tiger, not knowing how to get off without being eaten."*

He also claimed he hadn't taken a single rupee for himself from the inflated numbers. Investigators would later dispute that.

**VISUAL:** Trading screen, Mumbai. The Satyam share drops from about ₹179 to around ₹40.

**VO:**
That day, Satyam's shares lost almost 80 percent of their value. Billions in investor money vanished in hours.

And remember that governance trophy? It was taken back.

---

## ACT 5: THE FALL (8:30 – 10:00)

**VISUAL:** Police car silhouette. Jail bars slide across the screen.

**VO:**
Two days later, Ramalinga Raju was arrested.

The Indian government did something unusual. It dissolved Satyam's board and appointed its own directors to save the company. Not to save Raju, but to save the tens of thousands of employees and the clients depending on them.

**VISUAL:** "FOR SALE" sign on the Satyam tower. It changes to "Mahindra Satyam."

**VO:**
In 2009, Tech Mahindra won the bid to take over Satyam. The company survived, renamed Mahindra Satyam, and was later merged fully into Tech Mahindra. The name "Satyam" disappeared.

**VISUAL:** Gavel. Year counter: 2015.

**VO:**
The court case dragged on for six years. In 2015, a special court convicted Raju, his brother and several others, sentencing them to seven years in prison.

And the auditors? Satyam's accounts had been audited by PricewaterhouseCoopers, one of the biggest audit firms in the world. Its auditors were also convicted in India, and US regulators fined PwC's Indian affiliates millions of dollars for failing to catch the fraud.

---

## ACT 6: HOW DID NOBODY NOTICE? (10:00 – 10:50)

**VISUAL:** Checklist with three boxes, each getting a red X.

**VO:**
So how did a lie this big survive for years?

One: auditors trusted documents instead of checking with the banks directly.

Two: the board, including well-known independent directors, approved what the founder wanted.

Three: everyone was making money. When a share price keeps going up, very few people ask hard questions.

**VISUAL:** The paper tower from Act 2 collapses into a pile of pages.

**VO:**
Satyam's fall changed Indian business. Corporate governance rules were tightened, auditors faced new scrutiny, and "Satyam" became shorthand for corporate fraud in India.

---

## OUTRO (10:50 – 11:15)

**VISUAL:** Pages settle. The word *Satyam* fades, leaving only *truth*.

**VO:**
A company named after truth, built on one of the biggest lies in Indian business history.

It started as a small gap between real numbers and reported ones. And that's the lesson: frauds this big almost never begin big.

They begin small… and then they become a tiger you can't get off.

**VISUAL:** End screen: next-episode thumbnail + subscribe button.

**VO:**
Next time on Paper Empires: how the world's biggest phone company went from number one to nothing in six years.

Subscribe, and I'll see you there.
