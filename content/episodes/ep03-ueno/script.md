# Episode 3: The Bank That Toppled a President

**[cold open]**
At 3:24 in the morning on November 2nd, 2026, Paraguay's central bank posted a short message online.
*A dark city at night: rain, street lights, one lit window. A phone buzzes on a bedside table, and the post glows on screen.*

By sunrise, the most popular banking app in the country had stopped working.
*Thousands of phones across a map of Paraguay flick from green to grey, one after another.*

Three million customers, about six in ten adults, could not pay, withdraw cash, or use their cards.
*A crowd of little figures fills the screen; six out of every ten turn grey.*

Five days later, the President of Paraguay was out of office and under arrest.
*A presidential sash slides off a figure, and handcuffs click.*

How does one bank bring down a president?
*The bank tower and the presidential palace lean toward each other like dominoes.*

**[chapter 1 · The Bank in Everyone's Pocket]**
Ueno started in 1976 as a small finance company, and for decades almost nobody noticed it.
*A tiny shopfront from 1976 on a quiet street; the calendar flips.*

In 2021 a powerful business family, Grupo Vázquez, took control, renamed it Ueno, and turned it into an app.
*A family-tree logo plants a flag; the shopfront folds into a smartphone.*

It offered cashback on groceries, credit cards for people who had never had one, and its name on the shirts of the national football team.
*A shopping cart rains coins; a card slides into a farmer's hand; a football shirt spins with the logo.*

In 2023 it won a full banking licence. In 2024 it bought another bank, Visión Banco, and became the sixth-largest bank in the country.
*A licence stamp lands; two buildings merge into one; a podium ranks banks, with Ueno climbing to number 6.*

At the same time, Paraguay was selling itself to investors as the "Dubai of South America": low taxes, steady growth, and new investment-grade credit ratings.
*A skyline with gold sparkles, a palm-tree "Dubai" postcard, and ratings badges clicking on.*

---

**[chapter 2 · Friends in High Places]**
Paraguay's president, Santiago Peña, was a former IMF economist elected in 2023.
*A portrait card (licensed photo if available, otherwise a silhouette) and an "IMF" briefcase.*

Before and during his presidency, he held a small stake in Ueno's holding company. He said he sold it in 2025.
*A share certificate showing "1.58%" drifts away from his card, with a 2025 date stamp.*

He also picked Carlos Carvallo, who had led Ueno's predecessor company, to run the central bank: the regulator that was supposed to watch Ueno.
*A referee's whistle passes from the bank's hand to the central bank building; a "conflict?" chip pops up.*

And public money poured in. By August 2026, state agencies had about 850 million dollars at Ueno, including around 355 million from the national social security fund.
*Pension envelopes stream from a government building into the Ueno tower; a counter rolls to $850M.*

Paraguay's social security institute had loosened its own rules, which let it put more of its money there.
*A rulebook page is rubbed out with an eraser; the "limit" line slides up.*

---

**[chapter 3 · The Warnings]**
In June 2026, central bank inspectors flagged about 146 million dollars in risky loans to companies linked to the bank's own owners.
*An inspector's magnifier over a ledger; red lines link the loans back to the family-tree logo.*

That summer, the newspaper ABC Color started asking questions. Ueno threatened to sue.
*A newspaper slaps onto a desk; a legal letter flies back at it.*

In October, a leaked central bank report naming problems at Ueno reached the Senate. Peña criticised the leak, and Ueno questioned whether the document was genuine.
*A folder slides under a door marked SENATE; flashes from cameras; a "genuine?" chip.*

On paper, Ueno said it was healthy, with capital above the legal minimum. On October 30th, it announced a plan to raise 100 million dollars.
*A gauge reads 13.8%, safely above a 12% line. A "+$100M" banner unfurls.*

**[chapter 4 · The Night the App Went Dark]**
Three days later, the central bank shut it down.
*The bank tower at night: the lights go out floor by floor, top to bottom.*

Inspectors said that once bad loans were counted properly, Ueno's capital was not 13.8 percent. It was 5.56 percent, less than half the legal minimum.
*The gauge needle drops from 13.8 to 5.56 and the red 12% line flashes.*

Overnight, millions of accounts froze. Salaries, savings and pensions were stuck.
*Ice crystals spread across a bank card; a pay-slip, a piggy bank and a pension cheque freeze in blocks.*

Shops lost their payment systems. In some cities, a school meals programme was suspended.
*A card terminal blinks ERROR; a school lunch tray slides off a table.*

A state guarantee fund began paying depositors back. Officials said 97 percent of customers would be covered in full, but Moody's estimated only about a third of the money held at the bank was insured.
*Two charts side by side: "97% of people" vs "about 1/3 of the money".*

Paraguay's currency fell more than 8 percent against the dollar.
*A guaraní banknote shrinks against a growing dollar bill.*

---

**[chapter 5 · Five Days]**
Monday, November 3rd: the central bank chief resigned. Crowds gathered outside closed branches.
*A calendar page tears off: MON. A chair spins empty. Crowds outside shuttered doors.*

Then the crowds moved to Congress and the president's residence. Ueno's own staff burned their company cards. Police fired pepper spray and rubber bullets.
*A night march with banners and burning barricades (stylised, no gore); cards curl in a small fire.*

Thursday, November 5th: Peña said he would resign "for the stability of our country."
*A resignation letter on a desk, signed.*

But lawmakers refused. Resigning would have given him a lifetime Senate seat, and with it, immunity. The lower house voted 78 to 0 to impeach him instead.
*The letter is stamped REJECTED; a vote board fills 78–0.*

Friday, November 6th: the Senate voted 44 to 0 to remove him. Vice President Pedro Alliana was sworn in that evening.
*A second vote board 44–0; the sash passes to a new figure.*

That night, prosecutors ordered Peña's arrest.
*Police lights sweep across the residence gate.*

Over the weekend, he and the former central bank chief were charged with breach of trust. Prosecutors charged 27 people linked to the bank, and a judge ordered its founder and president detained.
*Case folders stack up, labelled 2, then 27; warrant papers stamp down.*

Investigators raided Peña's country house and seized luxury cars, private planes and a yacht.
*A raid montage: a door swings open, then a car, a plane and a yacht are tagged EVIDENCE.*

---

**[chapter 6 · What We Know]**
Peña denies any wrongdoing, and no one has been convicted.
*The scales of justice wobble; a "NOT CONVICTED" chip.*

Ueno has disputed the leaked report and the reporting against it.
*A "DISPUTED" stamp on the newspaper.*

How much money is truly missing is still being counted.
*An abacus rattles; question marks drift up.*

This has happened before. In the late 1990s, Paraguay closed 13 banks and 35 finance companies.
*A row of 1990s bank fronts shutting one by one, in sepia.*

---

**[outro]**
In five years, Ueno went from a small finance company to the bank in most people's pockets.
*The phone app grows from one phone to a sea of phones.*

In five days, it took a president down with it.
*The domino from the cold open falls: the bank topples the palace.*

## Fact check (sources)
- Timeline, solvency ratios (13.8% reported on 30 Sep, 5.56% adjusted), 3:24 a.m. announcement on 2 Nov, votes 78–0 and 44–0, Alliana sworn in, Peña's arrest, raids and seizures, guaraní −8%, 1990s crisis: Wikipedia, "2026 Paraguay banking crisis" (snapshot of 10 Nov), cross-checked with the sources below.
- About 3M customers (about 60% of adults), $146M in related-party loans, ~$800M public funds incl. ~$380M pension/health fund, six executives detained, 27 charged, protester quotes: Al Jazeera, 6 Nov 2026.
- "Dubai of South America", $850M public funds incl. $355M from IPS (about 9% of its portfolio), IPS rule changes 2023–24, Peña's 1.58% stake and 2025 exit, Carvallo's resignation on 3 Nov, staff burning cards, rubber bullets, 13 banks and 35 finance firms in 1995–98: The Conversation.
- Peña removed, charged, denies wrongdoing; Alliana serves until 2028: NPR (9 Nov), MercoPress (7 Nov), bne IntelliNews.
- Where sources differ, the script uses the safe version:
  - Deposit insurance cap ($16k–38k in different sources): no dollar figure is used.
  - Public funds ($800M vs $850M): "about $850M by August 2026", per The Conversation and Wikipedia.
  - Miguel Vázquez's title: "founder", as Al Jazeera uses.
  - Peña's house loan, the hidden wall and the ITTI contracts: left out (single-source or allegation-heavy).
